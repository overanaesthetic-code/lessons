Task 4.2 · Multiple choice

Always on: the rise of asynchronous communication

Reading passage · ~330 words

AFor most of the twentieth century, workplace communication meant either a face-to-face conversation or a synchronous phone call — something that happened in real time, with both people expected to respond immediately. Email, when it first entered offices, changed that expectation: for the first time on a large scale, professional correspondence could be asynchronous, sent and then answered hours or even days later. Today, most workplace messaging — chat platforms, project boards, documents with comments attached — follows the same asynchronous logic, and some researchers argue that this shift has changed white-collar work as much as the internet itself.

BAdvocates make a straightforward case. A synchronous culture, where every question demands an immediate reply, forces people to interrupt whatever they are doing to answer it, and interruptions are expensive. Studies of office workers have repeatedly found that it takes several minutes to fully return to a task after a single interruption; a single unnecessary phone call can cost far more than the two minutes it appeared to take. Asynchronous tools let a worker batch their replies, protecting long stretches of focused, uninterrupted time — increasingly called "deep work" — which many jobs depend on.

CCritics point to a different cost: ambiguity. A short message, stripped of tone of voice and facial expression, is far easier to misinterpret than a face-to-face remark, and a reply that takes six hours to arrive is easily read as a barrier rather than simply as someone working through their inbox in order. Teams that rely too heavily on delayed written messages often report more communication breakdowns, not fewer, because small misunderstandings are never caught and corrected in real time the way they would be in conversation. Some managers use the phrase "notification fatigue" to describe employees who, even outside working hours, feel obliged to reply almost instantly out of habit — quietly turning an asynchronous tool back into a synchronous one.

DThe organisations that seem to manage this best are not the ones that pick a single method and apply it everywhere, but the ones that choose a channel deliberately: a quick, low-stakes update goes to chat; anything ambiguous, sensitive, or likely to be misread moves to a call or a face-to-face conversation instead. Asynchronous communication, in other words, has not replaced synchronous communication so much as forced every organisation to decide, message by message, which one a given piece of information actually deserves.

05 · Speaking

Three voice messages — Part 1, Part 2, Part 3. Record each as one take and send it.

Part 1 · 3–4 minutes

Everyday communication

● record one voice message for all six

Two to three sentences each — answer, then add a reason or example.

  1. How do you usually communicate with your close friends?
  2. Do you prefer face-to-face conversations or talking on the phone? Why?
  3. How quickly do you usually reply to messages?
  4. Have you ever had a misunderstanding because of a text message or an email?
  5. Do you think people rely on their phones too much to communicate?
  6. What's the last app you used to talk to someone?
Part 2 · Long turn

Cue card

● record a separate voice message

Describe a time when a message or piece of information was misunderstood.

You should say:

  • what the message was
  • who was communicating with whom
  • what went wrong

and explain how the misunderstanding was eventually sorted out.

01:00 02:00
Language to build in

Setting it up: "It was a message about… sent between… / We were talking at cross purposes without realising it."

What went wrong: a barrier to communication · the instructions were ambiguous · it got completely lost in translation · a real communication breakdown

How it resolved: "In the end we touched base face-to-face and sorted it out in two minutes." · "Once she conveyed what she actually meant, it was obvious."

Part 3 · 4–5 minutes

Discussion

● record one voice message for all six

Longer, more abstract answers. Use a concede-then-state pattern where you can: "Admittedly… but I'd argue…"

Cluster A · Technology & communication

  1. How has technology changed the way people communicate compared to twenty years ago?
  2. Do you think written communication is replacing spoken communication?
  3. Could AI ever fully replace human communication in customer service?

Cluster B · Communication & relationships

  1. Is it possible to build a genuine relationship with someone you've never met face-to-face?
  2. Why do misunderstandings happen even between people who share a first language?
  3. Some people say the ability to communicate clearly matters more than intelligence. Do you agree?

Quick reminder

  • I'd argue that… — stating a view with weight
  • Admittedly…, but… — conceding, then countering
  • What strikes me most is… — emphasising your main point
  • I'm not entirely convinced that… — disagreeing politely
